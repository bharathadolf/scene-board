# Draft: the story told to someone who has never heard it

**Status: draft. Nothing here is final.** This is one proposal for how to
present the existing material to an audience with no knowledge of the culture
it comes from, without changing a single event in it.

---

## 1. The problem, precisely

An Indian viewer sees a dark-skinned man with a conch and a chariot and already
knows: he is divine, he is beloved, he will win, and whatever he does is
correct. That recognition is doing an enormous amount of the film's work for
free — it supplies the character before the film has to earn him.

Strip it out and look at what is left on screen in the current cut:

- A voice from the sky answers a petition in the fourth scene. To an outsider
  this reads as a story with no stakes, because something omnipotent is clearly
  managing it.
- Forty unnamed figures pull a mountain out of an ocean for six minutes before
  any human being has been introduced.
- A man wins seventeen sieges and releases the enemy each time, and the film
  does not explain why until very late. Without cultural trust in him, that is
  not a mystery — it is a character who seems to have no plan.
- Half of what the audience learns about him arrives as a bard, a priest, a
  visitor or a mural **telling** somebody. An outsider has no reason to care
  what a bard thinks.

So the fix is not simplification. It is **converting inherited reverence into
earned curiosity**: an outsider must be made to want the face that an Indian
audience already loves.

---

## 2. What we are not changing

Every event stays, in the form it already has. No scene gains an invented
motive, nobody behaves out of character, nothing is modernised, and the myth is
not rationalised or explained away. What changes is **order, point of view,
withholding, and whether a thing is spoken or shown.**

The four standing laws survive intact:

- Krishna is unseen until the temple door.
- Nothing follows the final episode.
- Rukmini is the protagonist.
- There is no wedding scene.

---

## 3. Seven rules of presentation

**1 · No god words in the first hour.** Not *Lord*, not *divine*, not
*Vāsudeva*, not *avatar*. Everybody on screen talks about him the way soldiers
talk about a commander they have never met. Divinity is delivered as
inexplicable *results* — an army that is gone, a city that was not there
yesterday — and never as vocabulary. The audience is allowed to conclude it.
Nobody is allowed to announce it.

**2 · Function before name.** Nobody is introduced by name. Jarasandha is *the
king who always comes back* for twenty minutes before he is Jarasandha.
Kālayavana is *the one who has come to kill him*. Śiśupāla is *the bridegroom*.
A name is given only once the audience has a reason to retain it.

**3 · One pair of eyes.** If Rukmini does not know it, the audience does not
know it — with two exceptions: the cold open, and the carriers (the
water-carrier, the potter, the ferryman), who see consequences she cannot.
Everything else she assembles, and the audience assembles with her.

**4 · Count, don't preach.** Seventeen chalk marks. Twenty-three divisions.
Thirty million men at the second gate. One road nobody is watching. One hour
named in a letter. A hundred offences owed. Numbers travel across every
culture; cosmology does not.

**5 · Objects, not exposition.** The film's load-bearing information is carried
by things an audience can see somebody hold: the chalk, the garland, the wax
seal, the dry letter, the forged relic, the lamp, the shaved hair, the empty
place at the table, the clean wall. The whole story can be followed by watching
objects change hands.

**6 · The withholding is a thriller device, not a ritual.** For an outsider,
"we never see his face" is not reverence — it is suspense, and it has to be
played that way. Every character who has seen him describes him differently.
Two witnesses contradict each other. A relic of him turns out to be a fake.
By the time the face arrives, the audience should be leaning forward to check
it against the evidence they have been given.

**7 · Argue, don't recite.** Information reaches the audience only through
three shapes: a listener who does not believe the speaker, two witnesses who
disagree, or an object that contradicts what is being said. No scene in which
somebody informs a respectful listener survives into this draft.

## 4. The three questions

Each act runs on one question an outsider can hold without any background, and
each is answered one act late.

| act | the question | answered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **I** | Who is the man who walked out of a city alone and unarmed, and why did somebody that size come to kill him? | end of act II, in Balarama's anger |
| **II** | Why does he beat this king seventeen times and let him go seventeen times? | act III, by what he does to her brother |
| **III** | She has been sold. What is she going to do about it? | the last shot |

## 5. Characterization

For each principal: what they want, the wound under it, the contradiction that
makes them interesting, how they are introduced *as an action*, and the line
they must never be given.

### Rukmini — the protagonist
**Wants:** to make one decision in her own life that is hers.
**Wound:** she is the most intelligent person in her house and has never once
been asked a question.
**Contradiction:** she is a sceptic who is in love with a rumour. She tests
every claim about him, catches a forger, disbelieves a sailor — and then stakes
her life on a letter.
**Introduced by:** correcting a soldier's arithmetic in a market. She knows the
tally of the sieges better than her brother does, and she corrects him in front
of people, and then immediately regrets being seen doing it.
**Never say:** anything that explains her feelings. She is shown writing,
checking, bribing a ferryman, hiding something in a fold of cloth.
**Her best scene:** she hears the one story about him that does not flatter him
— that there is a village still waiting for him — and she seals the letter
anyway.

### Krishna — the absence
He cannot be characterized by performance before the temple, so he is
characterized entirely by **decisions other people cannot understand**, each
shown as an act:

- He walks out of a defended city alone and unarmed, and the gate closes behind
  him. *(He arranges his own vulnerability.)*
- He is called a coward for three minutes by the man chasing him and does not
  turn around. *(He can be insulted and does not need to answer.)*
- He wins a battle and opens his brother's fist to let the enemy go.
  *(He is playing a longer game than anyone around him.)*
- He hands the wealth of a battlefield to his king and keeps none of it.
- He presses an old messenger's feet and asks about his health before he will
  look at the letter. *(Hospitality before his own interest — shot as hands.)*
- He leaves a village that is still, years later, waiting for him.
  *(The cost of him, carried by other people.)*

**Never say:** any statement of his own nature. The one place he describes
himself — the cave — is given to the king who hears it, with the voice kept
ambiguous: the king hears it, the audience may or may not.

### Rukmī — the brother
**Wants:** to be taken seriously by powerful men.
**Wound:** he is the son of a father who defers to him out of love, which he
has mistaken for having earned authority.
**Contradiction:** he sells his sister to buy standing, and the standing is
with men who will not remember his name a week later.
**Introduced by:** describing the king who always comes back with open
admiration, in front of a visitor, while she listens from the next room.
**Never say:** that he loves her. Show it once, badly timed — he straightens
her dress at the fitting and cannot look at her.

### Balarama — the brother who disagrees
**Wants:** his brother to behave like what he is.
**Contradiction:** he marches an army to defend a decision he thinks is wrong,
and then says so to his face in front of everyone.
**Introduced by:** having the enemy king by the throat with a rope in his hand,
and being told to let go.
**He carries the film's hardest job:** he is the one who finally explains the
seventeen releases — in anger, as an accusation, which is the only way the
audience will accept an explanation at all.

### Jarasandha — the king who always comes back
**Wants:** his dead son-in-law avenged, and his daughters' grief answered.
**Wound:** he cannot stop performing his grief in public.
**Contradiction:** he is a competent, courageous, well-loved commander, and he
is unable to learn one thing in seventeen years because his friends keep
telling him it was fate.
**Introduced by:** an army arriving on a horizon over the course of one day,
counted by a man with a bucket.
**Never say:** a villain speech. His biggest moment is consoling the man he
ruined, on a roadside, kindly.

### Śiśupāla — the bridegroom
**Wants:** what he was promised.
**Contradiction:** his humiliation is entirely passive — he is never fought,
never beaten, never addressed. He rides at the chariot alone, is passed over,
and turns back unhurt.
**Introduced by:** shaking hands over a woman in the room next to hers.
**Never say:** a threat he can carry out. Everything he promises is for later.

### The carriers — one collective character
The water-carrier, the potter, the flower vendor, the goatherd, the farmer, the
fisher family, the ferryman. They never learn the cause of anything. They are
the film's measuring instrument: the chalk marks, the first pot of a new city,
the garlands worn by a different bride, the clean wall at the end. **For an
outsider they are the way in** — they want what any audience wants, which is
for the week to be survivable.

## 6. Show instead of tell: ten replacements

The current cut delivers much of its information through recitals. Each is
replaced by a scene in which something happens. These are the ten new scenes
this draft needs; codes `D-01`–`D-10`.

| replaces | what it was | `D-` | what happens instead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E05-16 | a visitor lists the marks by which he is known | **D-01** | She is sold a relic in the market — a garland said to be his — and knows within four seconds that it is a fake, and says how she knows. *The audience receives the checklist of marks from a forger getting them wrong.* |
| E02-09 | visitors sing his praises, she listens | **D-02** | She brings two travellers who both claim to have been at Mathurā into the same room, asks one question, and catches one of them lying. *We learn what she is, and we get the siege from the one who was there.* |
| E03-14 | her brother talks politics at a visitor | **D-03** | She is measured for the wedding dress in one room while the sale is agreed in the next. No dialogue of hers. The tailor's hands, her brother's voice through a wall, her face. |
| E04-09 | sailors argue about a city in the sea | **D-04** | She pays a ferryman to row her out to the place the sailor swore it stands. Empty water to the horizon. She goes home and draws it anyway. |
| E05-15 | a priest recites a king's prayer | **D-05** | The temple keeps a relic: a pair of sandals and a crown left by a king who walked north and never came back for them. She picks up the crown. It is heavier than she expected. |
| E02-08 | an old woman tells the story of the weeping Earth | **D-06** | She watches it: a cow lies down in the road in front of an army, the soldiers curse it, and nobody can move it. No narration at all. |
| — | *(new strand)* | **D-07** | A messenger rides into a village a long way from anywhere, carrying a message from a man who left it. The village has not moved on. |
| — | *(new strand)* | **D-08** | A foster mother in that village still cooks for someone who is not coming. She feeds the messenger instead. She asks nothing about the man. |
| — | *(new strand)* | **D-09** | The messenger comes back with nothing to report except how much he is missed, and cannot say it. |
| — | *(new strand)* | **D-10** | That story reaches her, third-hand, two days before the wedding — the one piece of information about him that does not flatter him. She writes anyway. |

`D-07` to `D-10` are the single most valuable addition in this draft. An
outsider cannot love a man who has no cost attached to him, and this is the
cost: he leaves people behind, and she chooses him knowing it.

An outsider can retain about nine proper names in an hour. This draft spends
them like money.

| Krishna | *the man who walked out of the gate* | ≈ 26 min, by her, as a question |
| Kālayavana | *the one who came to kill him* | never named in act I; *the Yavana* |
| Mucukunda | *the sleeper* | ≈ 12 min, by himself |

| Rukmini | — | her own entry, ≈ 25 min |
| Rukmī | *her brother* | ≈ 35 min |
| Śiśupāla | *the bridegroom* | ≈ 37 min |
| Balarama | *the brother with the plough* | ≈ 17 min |
| Dvārakā | *the city in the sea* | ≈ 53 min |

Everything else — the Devas, the Asuras, Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Lakṣmī, Dhanvantari,
Vāsuki, Kūrma — is never named on screen in this draft. They appear once, in
the mural sequence, as figures in a painted story, which is how a stranger
would meet them.

## 8. The new order

Twenty sequences, ≈ 175 minutes. Interval at 95. **The face arrives at minute
126** — earlier than the master cut's 171, because a withhold that long needs
cultural trust to survive and this version does not have any. Action runs at
about 42%.

The big structural move: **the Churning leaves the front of the film.** Instead
of opening on cosmology it arrives at minute 111, as a painted wall she is
standing in front of while she decides what to do — so the audience meets the
myth after they are invested, and reads it as her mirror rather than as
homework.

## 11. What the Indian audience gets instead

This draft does not take the myth away from the audience that already knows it;
it moves it from the front to the middle and turns recognition into a second
layer.

- The mural sequence at minute 111 is, for a viewer who knows, a complete
  retelling of the Churning — and they will see what the panels mean two beats
  before the stranger does.
- The bard at the wedding recites a story in which the kings in the hall are
  the ones who grabbed the pot. An Indian audience gets that joke; an outsider
  gets a good scene.
- The garland, the conch, the plough, the chalk, the lamp all work as objects
  for a stranger and as iconography for everyone else.
- Withholding the face for 126 minutes is a far bigger gift to an audience who
  knows that face than to one that does not.

## 12. Open questions for the next pass

1. **Is 126 minutes still too long to withhold the face** for a cold audience,
   or is the evidence engine strong enough to hold it? The alternative is the
   commercial-cut position: show him in the first ten minutes and spend the
   film on *her*.
2. **Does act I survive without her for 25 minutes?** Her entry is currently
   late. Moving `NEW-03` to minute 8 would cost the cave its silence.
3. **The voice in the cave** — heard by the king only, or by the audience? This
   is the single decision that determines whether the film is a fantasy or a
   mystery.
4. **Is `D-07`–`D-09` a strand or a single scene?** Six minutes of a village
   that is still waiting is the best characterization in the draft and the
   easiest thing for a producer to cut.
5. **Where does the seventeen-release explanation actually land** — Balarama in
   anger at minute 156, or earlier, in `E03-03`, where this draft deliberately
   makes it inaudible? The draft bets on holding it for two hours. That may be
   an hour too long.
6. **Does the flood (`A1`) replace or join the seventeen montage?** Both is
   probably too much siege.
7. **Act III runs 45 minutes with him visible** (130–175). Is that the right shape after
   two hours of absence, or does the film need one more scene of him not being
   seen after the reveal?
8. **How much Telugu-language texture survives** the decision to play
   everything as fiction — names, honorifics, the bard's metre? None of it is
   on the table in this draft and all of it should be.
