# The story

*A feature film. Working English title: **The Unseen Sun**.*

**A note on the title.** *Loka Kalyāṇam* (లోక కళ్యాణం) means the welfare of the
world, and it is this film's **theme**, not its title and not a Telugu
translation of *The Unseen Sun* — the two phrases have never had anything to do
with each other, and earlier files in this project paired them as if they did.
If a Telugu title is wanted, it should be chosen on its own merits. Four that
would work: **అదృశ్య సూర్యుడు** (*Adṛśya Sūryuḍu*, "the unseen sun"), which
carries the English title across; **రుక్మిణి లేఖ** (*Rukmiṇi Lēkha*,
"Rukmini's letter"), which names the actual engine of the plot;
**పదిహేడు** (*Padihēḍu*, "seventeen"), after the seventeen sieges, which is the
most commercial of the four; or **లోక కళ్యాణం** kept deliberately as the
title, in which case it should be understood as a claim about the story's
subject and not as a rendering of the English.

---

## What it is

A woman in an inland kingdom is being sold to a man she has never met, to buy
her brother standing with a king he admires. She has never been asked a
question in her life. Over several years she assembles, out of market rumour and
soldiers' gossip and one painted wall, a picture of a man she has never seen —
and then, eight days before her wedding, she writes him a letter telling him
where to be and when.

That is the whole plot. Everything else in the film is the world that letter
has to cross.

---

## The world before

Long before any of it, two armies who hate each other are made to work
together. Heaven has been cursed and stripped — its prosperity gone, its
soldiers weak — and the only thing that can restore it lies at the bottom of an
ocean. The gods are told to make an alliance with their enemies, put a mountain
in the water as a churning rod, and turn it.

They do. It takes an age. The mountain sinks and is lifted back up on something
enormous and alive. A serpent is used as the rope. Both sides pull until their
hands split, and neither lets go, and neither understands what is holding the
mountain up.

The first thing the ocean gives them is not the prize. It is poison — a black
stain crossing milk-white water faster than a man can run, killing everything
it touches. The armies drop the rope and run. One god walks down into it, drinks
it, and holds it in his throat while his wife's hand closes around his neck to
keep it there. Then the churning starts again.

After that the treasures come up, and every single one of them is grabbed. A
cow, a horse, an elephant, a jewel, a tree — each claimed by somebody who was
certain it was his. And then a goddess rises out of the water holding a garland,
and every god and every demon on that shore arranges himself to be chosen. She
walks the line. She looks at each of them and passes him. At the end she stops
in front of the one man who never stepped forward, and she puts the garland on
him.

The ones who were passed over are given wine as a consolation, and they swallow
it. When the nectar of immortality finally surfaces, they seize the pot — and
start fighting each other over who drinks first before anyone has drunk at all.
They lose it to a woman they hand the ladle to themselves. One of them sneaks
into the other line to steal a mouthful and loses his head for it.

Nothing in this film is explained twice, so it is worth saying once here: that
is the whole story in miniature. **Grabbing is not keeping. The one who waits is
chosen. And somebody always has to drink the poison.**

## The weight

Much later, and much lower down, the Earth is being ridden into the ground.
Armies cross farmland because crossing is quicker than going round. Crops go
under, wells are fouled, and a cow kneels in a field under the shadow of a
passing column.

The Earth, in the shape of that cow, goes and complains. The answer comes before
anyone has finished asking: the weight will be lifted, and it will be lifted by
a child who is going to be born into a particular family, in a particular city,
on the human side of the world — with his elder brother sent ahead of him.

## Seventeen years

The child grows up, and the first thing he does as a man is kill the tyrant who
had been terrorising that city. The tyrant's two wives go home to their father,
a king called Jarasandha, and tell him how their husband died. Inside one scene
his grief becomes an army.

He brings twenty-three divisions and rings the city. And the man inside does
something nobody can follow: he destroys the army, takes the king alive — and
then lets him go.

He does it again the next time. And the next. The enemy comes back with a new
plan every year — a night assault, fire carts, elephants, siege towers, a river
turned loose on the lower town — and every year the result is identical, and
every year the king is released to go home and raise another army. A
water-carrier on the wall chalks one mark after each siege. The marks reach
seventeen.

Nobody is told why. His own brother asks, is given a reason, and refuses to
accept it. The reason will not be said out loud again until the very end of the
film, in an argument, in anger: **he is letting the enemy gather the whole
world's arrogance into one pair of hands, because it is easier to lift a
burden that somebody else has helpfully piled up in one place.**

While that war grinds on, a second one arrives. A foreign king brings thirty
million men to the other gate, and the city is suddenly between two armies. So
the man stops defending it. He goes to the coast, points at the water, and a
city comes up out of the sea — walls, gates, towers, treasuries — and in a
single night his whole people are moved into it while they sleep. They go to bed
inland and wake up beside an ocean.

Then, with everyone safe inside it, he walks out through the gate alone and
unarmed, and the gate closes behind him.

The foreign king, who has been given a description of him, recognises him and
gets off his horse to kill him with his hands. What follows is a chase in which
the man being chased picks the route: always one stride out of reach, never far
enough to be lost, across fields and a river and up into the hills, while his
pursuer screams *coward* at his back and he lets it be said. He goes into a
cave. The pursuer follows him into the dark, finds a man asleep on the floor,
decides this is his quarry pretending, and kicks him.

The sleeper opens his eyes. He is a king who was granted undisturbed sleep and
the power to burn whoever woke him, and he has been asleep for ages. The
pursuer is ash on the floor in half a second — the largest kill in the story,
and the man who arranged it never raises a hand. The old king walks out into the
light, looks at the one who led his killer to him, is offered anything he wants,
refuses all of it, and goes north into the mountains to pray.

It still is not over. Jarasandha comes back with the twenty-third division while
the city's wealth is on the road, and this time the two brothers have no army
with them. They run. They run on foot, with cavalry filling the road behind
them, and they climb a mountain because there is nowhere else to go. The king
cannot find them, so he sets the hillside on fire. Fire runs up through the
trees faster than two men can climb; they get above the smoke; they run out of
mountain. Then they jump. Nobody finds any bodies in the ash, so the king
decides he has won, and goes home satisfied.

## The woman in Kuṇḍina

All of that has been reaching a young woman a long way inland, in pieces, for
years, and this is the part of the film that matters.

A soldier back from the north tells a merchant they beat that king seventeen
times and let him go seventeen times; she stops buying flowers and asks why, and
he does not know. A herdsman at a well says a giant ran into a cave after a man
and only smoke came out; she asks which man, and then she asks whether he was
wearing a garland. A sailor on a ferry swears there is a city standing in the
sea; she pays the ferryman to row her out to look, finds empty water to the
horizon, goes home and draws the city anyway. A trader in her father's hall
recites the marks by which the man is known. And at the temple, on a painted
wall, she keeps stopping at the same panel: a goddess with a garland, walking
past a long line of men who were sure.

She is clever, she tests everything, she catches people lying — and she is in
love with a rumour. Then her brother sells her. He has arranged a marriage to
the prince of a neighbouring kingdom in order to stand closer to the king he
admires, and their father, who would have chosen otherwise, gives way out of
love for his son. She is measured for the wedding clothes in one room while it
is agreed in the next.

So she does the one thing nobody has thought to guard against. She sends for a
priest she trusts and she writes: *I have chosen you. No sensible woman who has
heard what I have heard would choose another. If you do not come, I will not
live. Come in secret, and here is how — the procession to the temple, the
morning of the wedding.* She names the hour. She seals it and hands it away.

An old man walks that letter across a country, past a guard post, over a river
in flood, and ahead of a rider sent to catch him. It is read. The reader asks
one question — when is the wedding — and calls for his chariot, and rides
through the night.

## The taking

By then her wedding has become a war. The groom's side arrives with an army;
nine or ten kings arrive with theirs and swear aloud that if the man comes for
the bride they will fight him for her. The roads are held. The man's own
brother, certain this is going to get him killed, marches an army of his own to
be there.

She is bathed and dressed and walked to the temple inside a ring of soldiers.
She prays, asks the goddess for the one thing she wants, takes the blessing,
breaks her silence, and walks out through a forecourt full of kings — and finds
him. **It is the first time the audience sees his face, after nearly three
hours.** It takes four seconds and nobody says anything.

He takes her up in front of all of them, and her foot is already on the chariot
board, because she decided this eight days ago. Then he drives out through the
middle of them. Her bridegroom rides at the chariot alone and is simply passed
over — not fought, not disarmed, not looked at — and turns back unhurt, which is
worse. A rear-guard holds a river crossing so the chariot can get an hour. The
kings who swore the oath come apart on open ground.

Only her brother will not stop. He swears in front of everyone that he will not
go home until he has killed the man and brought his sister back, and he follows
them alone. Every weapon he lifts is broken. He jumps down with a sword, and the
sword is cut to pieces, and then the other sword comes up — and she goes down at
her husband's feet and asks for her brother's life.

She gets it, and it costs her. Instead of killing him, the man ties him with
cloth and shaves off half his hair and his moustache, and lets him go: mercy and
humiliation in one stroke. Her brother cannot show his face at home after that,
so he builds a city on a river plain and stays there. Elsewhere, on a roadside,
old Jarasandha sits with the ruined bridegroom and consoles him: victory and
defeat belong to time, and time will turn.

The man's own brother cuts her brother free and turns on him: *this was not
worthy of our family.* He gets no answer. Then he sits down beside her and tells
her, gently, that people carry their own joy and sorrow, and that a warrior's
duties are sometimes ugly, and she wipes her face.

## The sea

She has lived inland all her life, so the ocean arrives all at once from a
moving chariot, with a city standing in it, and she measures it against the
drawing she made from a sailor's story.

The city is at its doors. And in the doorways are all the ordinary people the
film has been following for three hours without ever letting them understand
what they were part of: the water-carrier who chalked seventeen marks, the
potter who turned the first pot of a new city, the flower vendor, the goatherd,
the farmer whose strip of land is finally ploughing clean, the fisher family who
once pulled up a net full of silver and never knew why. A cow lies down in the
middle of the street and nobody needs to move her. The wall is clean.

In her new room she puts down the lamp she carried from home and finds another
one already lit. In the middle of the celebration there is a place laid for a
brother who is not coming. In a courtyard a singer is performing the story of
the abduction, as the whole country will perform it for centuries — and he
includes the letter, which makes it hers.

There is no wedding scene. The film ends on the two of them at the edge of the
water, with her at the centre of the frame, and then it stops.

## What it is about

Three things, and the film says all three with pictures rather than lines.

**One.** The world is not put right from the top. It is put right by a woman
with a pen, an old priest with sore feet, and a man who refuses to win quickly
— and the people who benefit never find out. The largest act of welfare in the
film is a girl's letter; the largest kill belongs to somebody who was asleep.

**Two.** Taking is not keeping. Everyone in this story who grabs — the demons
with the pot, the brother with his sister, the kings with their oath, the
bridegroom with his contract — ends up holding nothing. The only person who
gets what she wants is the one who chose it and asked for it in writing.

**Three.** Somebody has to drink the poison. The god at the shore, the man who
walks out of his own gate unarmed, the woman who kneels and asks for the life
of a brother who will hate her for it. Mercy in this film is never free and
never clean, and the film is careful to show the bill: a shaved head, an empty
chair, a brother in exile, an enemy still alive and already being told that time
will turn.

## Whose story it is

Rukmini's. He is the subject of the story and she is its protagonist, and the
film holds that distinction with one rule: **he is not seen until she sees
him.** For nearly three hours he is a conch, a hand at the edge of frame, a
silhouette against fire, an empty plain where an army used to be, and a
description given to the man who came to kill him. The audience assembles him
out of evidence at exactly the rate she does, and gets his face in the same
second she does.

Which is why the ending is a woman looking at the sea and not a wedding.
